California · calling about autism services

Four Questions First

Most of a first phone call gets spent on things that turn out not to matter, and the one question that decides how long everything takes usually never gets asked. These four, in this order, are the ones that change what happens next.

Before you dial anyone

1
Call the regional centre before you call providers.

Eligibility is what unlocks funding, and it runs on its own clock. Under three, ask for an Early Start intake — no diagnosis and no doctor's referral needed. Three or older, ask to apply for Lanterman eligibility.

2
Put the school request in writing, separately, and date it.

The school district process is independent of the regional centre. In California the district has 15 days to respond to a written assessment request and 60 days to complete the assessment. The date on your letter is what starts that clock.

3
Then call providers — with these four questions.

Have a pen. You will call more than one, and by the fourth call you will not remember which of them said what.

The four questions

Question one · ask it first

“Do you bill the regional centre directly, or do I need an insurance denial before they'll fund it?”

This is the question almost nobody knows to ask, and it is the one that costs the most time when it goes unasked. In California the regional centre is the payer of last resort. For a child of three or over it generally cannot fund behavioural services until private insurance or Medi-Cal has been billed and has said no — in writing.

Families who don't know this spend a month or two bouncing between two systems, each waiting for the other. Asking on the first call tells you which letter you need and who has to write it.

Good signThey explain the order without hesitating, and know which form the denial has to be on.
Ask more“We just take whatever the regional centre sends.” Then ask who submits to insurance first — you or them.

Question two

“Are you taking new clients this month? If not, when — and does your waitlist actually move?”

Every directory, ours included, tells you an organisation exists. None of them can tell you whether it has room, because that changes weekly and nobody publishes it. A federal secret-shopper study of mental health directories found the large majority of listed providers unreachable or not actually accepting patients.

The second half of the question matters as much as the first. A waitlist that has not moved in a year is a closed door with a friendlier name.

Good signA specific month, or a specific number of families ahead of you.
Ask more“We'll put you on the list.” Ask how long the last family waited.

Question three

“Which plans do you take — and do you take Medi-Cal?”

Ask them, not the directory. Health plans are required to keep their provider lists current and are routinely found not to be, so the plan's own website is the least reliable place to learn this. Name your plan out loud; “we take most insurance” is not an answer.

Ask about Medi-Cal by name even if you have private coverage. Many children qualify through a route their parents never checked, and a provider who takes it is a provider who can keep seeing your child if your work situation changes.

Good signThey name your plan back to you, and say whether they are in-network or out.
Ask more“We can bill anything.” Billing is not the same as being covered. Ask what you would pay.

Question four

“What ages do you serve — and is there anyone there who speaks [your language]?”

Age is not the formality it sounds like. In the regional centre vendor lists we have read, the organisations funded to serve children under three and those funded to serve children over three are almost entirely different organisations. Confirming the age range on the first call saves building a relationship you will have to leave.

On language: ask whether the clinician speaks it, not whether the office can find an interpreter. Those are different services and only one of them helps your child.

Good signA clear range, and a named person for the language.
Ask more“We can arrange interpretation.” Ask whether the person doing the therapy speaks it.

If your child is turning three

Ask this of whoever is treating your child now — before the birthday, not after.

“Will you still be able to see my child after they turn three? If not, who takes over, and who pays?”

Across the fifteen California regional centre vendor lists we have been able to read, the organisations funded for therapy under three and those funded for the same therapy at three and over barely overlap — a few dozen out of well over a thousand. Supply does not collapse at three. Continuity does. Most families change providers at that birthday, and the ones who find out in advance are the ones who don't lose months to it.

Three things move at once in that month: the eligibility bar rises from a delay in one area to substantial limitations in three, the payer shifts from the regional centre to your insurer and your school district, and your provider relationship most likely ends. Ask early enough that you are choosing rather than reacting.

Your calls

One card per place you ring. Write down what you heard and the date you heard it — an answer about openings is only true for about a month.

California-specific points here follow the state's own published rules: the regional centre as payer of last resort under Welfare & Institutions Code section 4659; the school district's 15-day and 60-day assessment timelines under Education Code section 56043; Early Start intake without a diagnosis or referral. The age-three pattern is our own reading of the vendor lists fifteen of California's twenty-one regional centres publish. None of this is legal advice, and a regional centre service coordinator or your local Family Resource Center can tell you how it applies to your child.
