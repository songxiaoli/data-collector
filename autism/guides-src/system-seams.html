California · autism services

The Handoffs Nobody Owns

Every institution in California’s autism system has a beginning and an end. The family is the only party whose responsibility runs the whole length — and it is the only party that was never assigned the job.

This is a map of the seams: the points where one body’s duty stops before the next one’s starts. For each, what the law actually says, what the state’s own numbers show happens instead, who currently stands in the gap, and what could be put there. Every figure is from a primary source and cited; where California does not publish a number, that is recorded as the finding rather than estimated.

Compiled 7 September 2026 · from California’s published files

California already has a law assigning someone to make sure a child actually receives what their plan promised. It is W&I §4647: the regional centre service coordinator is responsible for “securing” the services in the plan and “monitoring implementation of the plan to ascertain that objectives have been fulfilled.”

The same code sets that coordinator’s caseload at an average of 1:62, with an individual ceiling of 79 children permitted for up to 60 days (§4640.6). A regional centre that misses the ratio for two consecutive reporting periods receives technical assistance and a plan of correction.

So the delivery-assurance job exists, is named in statute, and is given to one person holding sixty-two children — inside the same agency whose purchasing decision is often the reason the service did not arrive. Everything below follows from that.

The seams, in the order a family meets them

01

Noticing something, and reaching anyone

The law says

Anyone may refer a child to Early Start — including the parent, with no doctor and no diagnosis. The regional centre must complete assessment and hold the first plan meeting within 45 calendar days. Evaluation, assessment and service coordination are free, by federal requirement, at every income level.

What happens

Caregivers notice a difference at 1.75 years, raise it with someone at 2.50, and reach a diagnosis at 4.20 (Martin et al., Frontiers in Psychiatry 2026). Latino children are diagnosed about 2.5 years later than white non-Latino children (Zuckerman, Academic Pediatrics 2014).

Nothing in that gap is a violation. Screening at 18 and 24 months is a professional recommendation, not a duty with a consequence, and no party is assigned the job of telling a worried parent that the door is already open to them.

Who stands here now

Early Start Family Resource Centers — free, staffed by parents of children with disabilities, and open to a family whether or not the child is eligible for anything. That last clause is the most useful unknown fact in the system.

But they are not an entitlement. Gov. Code §95024 says the lead agency may allocate funds to family resource services out of the federal Part C money remaining after the mandatory components are paid. High priority, discretionary residue. Nothing requires one per catchment.

What would close it

Not a directory — a sentence, delivered where the worry starts. “You can refer your own child today. It costs nothing. They have 45 days.” That closes most of this seam on its own. The hard part is distribution: it has to reach a paediatric waiting room and a WhatsApp group, not a website that assumes you already knew to search.

02

The third birthday

The law says

California is stricter than the federal floor. 17 CCR §52112 requires the regional centre to notify the school district and hold the transition meeting before the child is two years and nine months, with the referral to the district sent by that same date. Federal 34 CFR §303.209 requires the conference not fewer than 90 days before the third birthday.

At three, the eligibility test changes from a 25% delay in one area to significant limitations in two major life activities (provisional, ages 3–5) to substantial limitations in three or more for full Lanterman eligibility.

And the payer flips. W&I §4659(d)(1): a regional centre “shall not purchase medical or dental services for a consumer three years of age or older unless the regional center is provided with documentation of a Medi-Cal, private insurance, or a health care service plan denial.”

What happens

As of February 2025 California had 6,929 Early Start children still on the books past their third birthday — a 22.5% increase in one year (DDS Directive D-2025-Early Start-001).

The US Department of Education rated California’s Part C programme “Needs Intervention” in June 2025, with a score of 35.42% — and zero on both transition indicators, because the data were unreliable. The state cannot currently measure its own handoff.

In our own reading of the vendor lists across the regional centres that publish them, 628 organisations are reachable for birth-to-three therapy and 465 at three and over — but only 4 appear in both. Supply falls 26%; continuity falls to nothing. A family will almost certainly change providers at three, not because supply vanished but because the two workforces are different organisations doing different work under different billing codes.

Who stands here now

The service coordinator, on paper. The Family Resource Center does transition support — and then its own mandate ends, because the FRC serves families of children under 36 months. The one body that has been helping you is aged out on the same day your child is.

What would close it

A date, not a database. At two years and six months, tell the family exactly what must already have happened by two years and nine months, who is supposed to have done it, and what one sentence to say if it has not. This is the single place in the whole system where a checklist beats a directory, because no database can answer the question that actually matters — will the person treating my child still be treating them in four months, and who pays — and a prompt to ask it in time changes the outcome.

03

Between “authorised” and “received”

The law says

W&I §4647(b): “The regional center shall assign a service coordinator who shall be responsible for implementing, overseeing, and monitoring each individual program plan.” This is the delivery guarantee, and it exists.

What happens

24.7% of regional centre clients aged 3–21 received $0 in purchased services in FY2024–25 — 66,618 of 269,992 people. For ages birth to two the same figure is 3.2%.

Statewide, only 67% of authorised service dollars were actually spent; for ages 3–21 it was 56.6% (DDS Annual Purchase of Service Report, FY2024-25). That 43-point gap is the measurable footprint of services that exist in a plan and never reach a child.

A plan listing a service the child never received is not an edge case. It is a quarter of the school-age caseload.

Who stands here now

Nobody, in practice. The person statutorily assigned to monitor implementation is carrying sixty-two children, works for the agency whose purchasing decision is frequently the reason the service did not arrive, and the consequence for a regional centre that breaches the ratio for two consecutive reporting periods is a plan of correction.

What would close it

This is the gap where family-reported data has a value nobody can buy — and note that the question is not “does this provider have an opening.” It is “did the service written in your plan actually happen?” One question, asked of people who already know the answer, about a number the state publishes only in aggregate and a year late.

It is also the gap that most demands we talk to families before building anything, because the wrong wording on that question is worse than not asking it.

04

Three payers, no referee

The law says

The health plan must cover behavioural health treatment for autism (SB 946 → Health & Safety Code §1374.73) — but the mandate binds plan contracts and insurance policies, and a self-funded employer plan is neither. Federal ERISA preemption puts it out of California’s reach entirely (29 U.S.C. §1144). In California, 41.7% of private-sector enrollees at establishments that offer coverage are in self-insured plans — 58.2% at firms of 1,000 or more (AHRQ MEPS-IC 2020).

The regional centre may not purchase a service available from a health plan where the family “chooses not to pursue that coverage” (W&I §4659(c)). It may pay a copay or deductible — but only under 400% of the federal poverty level (§4659.1).

The school district owes only what the child needs to access their education.

What happens

Each party can point at another, correctly, under its own statute. There is no arbiter with jurisdiction over all three. The family is the integration layer.

Where the dispute is with a health plan there is a real remedy, and it works: of 5,204 Independent Medical Reviews closed in 2024, 51% were overturned by the reviewers and a further 22% were reversed by the plan before the review even happened — about 73% of families got the service (DMHC 2024 Annual Report). It is free.

But a family must know which regulator covers their plan, and DMHC publishes no way to find out. You discover you filed with the wrong agency by filing with the wrong agency.

Who stands here now

DMHC’s Help Center, for the plans it regulates — free, and it recovered $9.3m for members in 2024. It has no jurisdiction over the regional centre or the district, and none at all over a self-funded plan.

What would close it

Two things, both pure information. A three-question triage that tells a family which regulator they are actually under before they spend 30 days in a plan grievance. And this sentence, which may be the most actionable in this entire document: more than one in five denials evaporate the moment an outside reviewer is assigned. Those were services denied to families who, had they not filed, would simply have gone without.

05

Knowing that you can say no

The law says

Two deadlines, and they are not the same one. 60 days from the Notice of Action to appeal at all. 30 days, and before the action takes effect, to keep the services running while you appeal (DDS, Aid Paid Pending fact sheet). A family that files on day 35 keeps its appeal and loses its services in the meantime.

A rights complaint under W&I §4731 goes to the director of the regional centre being complained about, who has 20 working days to propose a resolution — and if the family does not escalate, that proposed resolution takes effect on the 20th working day by default.

What happens

Appeals work. Of 427 eligibility issues raised, 141 families were found eligible and 86 got the assessment they had been refused, against 108 found ineligible — roughly 53% ended with the family getting what had been denied (DDS Appeal Requests and Resolutions Report, FY2021-22).

Per-capita appeal rates vary 19-fold between regional centres — from 0.74 to 13.91 per thousand clients. That is not a difference in who has cause to appeal.

Rights complaints rose 185% from FY2020-21 to FY2023-24. Hispanic families are 43.9% of the caseload and file 34% of complaints.

And DDS has published no appeal outcomes since FY2021-22, so the success rate that would persuade a family to file is four years old and getting older.

Who stands here now

OCRA — free, one clients’ rights advocate per regional centre, and independent of it by statute. But OCRA states plainly that it weighs the merits, whether you could self-advocate, and resource availability before taking a case: “CRAs can only accept a limited caseload.” Most families get advice, not representation. And OCRA is funded by DDS, the department that oversees the centres it argues against.

What would close it

A 19-fold spread is an information gap, not a grievance gap. The fix is not a campaign — it is getting one date in front of one family in the right fortnight. Everything needed is already public; nothing has assembled it into something a parent reads on the day the letter arrives.

06

Twenty-two

The law says

Nothing, in the sense that matters. The educational entitlement ends. Adult services are discretionary and rationed. No party inherits the child.

What happens

26% of autistic young adults received no services at all in their early twenties. 58% had ever worked for pay after high school — against 92% for learning disability and 84% for speech-language impairment. 37% were “disconnected”: never worked, never continued education, against 7–8% for those other groups (Drexel National Autism Indicators Report, 2015).

The share of families reporting that obtaining services required significant effort rises from 50% at age 17 to over 70% after high school. About 80% of California’s autism caseload is under 22 — the cliff is the shape of the system, not an event at its edge.

Who stands here now

The regional centre remains, for those who hold full Lanterman eligibility. Per-consumer spending at 22+ is where the equity gap is widest in the entire system: $70,269 for white consumers against $39,824 for Hispanic consumers.

What would close it

Nothing on this page. Everything above is written for a parent, and the person on this side of twenty-two is an autistic adult, not a parent — a different reader, needing a different thing, from someone who has asked them what it is. It is named here so that its absence is a decision rather than an oversight, and so that anyone building for adults knows that the cliff is where the caseload already is, not a distant edge of it.

Four things that run through all of it

Inequity is manufactured at the seam

While the service is a federal entitlement, California is fair. The moment it becomes discretionary, it is not. This is a property of the design, not a story about individual bias — and it means the leverage is at the transition, not in the attitudes of staff.

Language is an identification failure

16% of the caseload speaks Spanish at home; Vietnamese, Mandarin and Cantonese are each under 1% — which is under-identification, not lower incidence. Telehealth, the usual answer to distance, was found to “disadvantage those with limited English proficiency”: interpreters were hard to integrate and platforms were English-only (Katakis et al., PLOS Digital Health 2025). It solves geography and worsens language.

The caregiver is not in the system

The system’s client is the child. Mothers of autistic children earn 56% less than mothers of children with no health limitation and work seven fewer hours a week; there is no significant effect on fathers (Cidav, Pediatrics 2012). 31% have a depressive disorder and 33% an anxiety disorder (Schnabel, Autism 2020). 66% of families report material hardship, and it peaks at ages 3–5 — exactly when parents know least.

The largest paid help is the least explained

IHSS Protective Supervision pays a parent to watch a child who cannot assess danger — up to 283 hours a month. Counties are required to explain it during assessment, which makes its obscurity a compliance failure rather than a design choice. Appeals take 90 days, benefits continue during them, and are not treated as an overpayment if the family loses. Almost nobody is told that last part.

What California does not measure about itself

  • How many children lose all services at age three. Not published — and the state’s own transition data were rated unreliable by the federal government, so it could not answer if asked. Do not let anyone put a number on this one.
  • Autism or ABA denial rates by health plans. Not published by DMHC, DHCS or CDI. The Independent Medical Review determinations file on the state open-data portal — every decision since 2001 — would yield an overturn rate, which is a different and arguably sharper number, but it has no denominator of denials.
  • Speech-language and occupational therapy vacancy rates. Not measured by the state workforce agency. The speech-language pathology licence file the state published is an empty archive.
  • Regional centre appeal outcomes since FY2021-22. Nothing published since June 2023.
  • Hours families spend on administration — appeals, IEP preparation, prior authorisations, waitlists. Never studied, by anyone. The nearest federal figure does not break out autism.

What would close them

Already public — and assembled nowhere

  • The referral sentence. You can refer your own child, today, free, and they have 45 days.
  • The two-years-nine-months alarm. What must have happened by then, and what to say if it has not.
  • The 30-day line. The deadline that keeps services running is not the deadline to appeal, and it is the shorter one.
  • Which regulator are you under. Three questions, before a family burns 30 days at the wrong door — and the fact that a fifth of denials fold on filing.
  • Protective Supervision, and that appealing it is close to risk-free.

Every one of these is a fact California already publishes. Not one of them requires new data, a budget, or anyone’s permission. What they require is arriving in front of one family in the right fortnight — which is a distribution problem, not a research problem, and is the reason a 19-fold spread in appeal rates exists between counties that are subject to identical law.

Needs someone families will answer honestly

  • Did the service in your plan actually happen? A quarter of the school-age caseload would answer no, and the state publishes that only in aggregate, a year late.
  • Who is taking families this month. No public source carries it and no directory in the country shows a verified version.

Neither can be bought, scraped or inferred. Both depend on families choosing to tell someone — which means the terms under which they are asked matter more than the software, and are not recoverable once set wrong.

Only California can do it, and has not

  • Publish what happens to children at three, with data the federal government will accept.
  • Publish denial and overturn rates by service category, so autism is separable.
  • Resume publishing appeal outcomes.
  • Fund delivery assurance — that is, staff §4647 as if it meant what it says.

Named here because a document that lists only what a small organisation could do quietly implies the rest is nobody’s fault. Four of the five things this system does not measure about itself are things it could begin measuring next year.
