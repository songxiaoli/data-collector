Who Does What

There are nine organisations in your child’s life and it is genuinely hard to tell them apart. Here is the shortcut: each one does exactly one of four things. Once you know which, most of the confusion goes away.

The ones who pay never do the work. The ones you go to when you are told no cannot make anything happen — they can only tell someone else to.

Almost every phone call that goes nowhere is one of those two facts arriving late.

How they connect to each other — and mostly don’t

The ones who pay

The regional centre

A non-profit that buys services with state money. California has 21. One of them is yours, based on where you live.

Under three
It is the payer, and this is the easiest the system will ever be. Anyone can refer your child — including you. No diagnosis needed. It costs nothing.
Three and over
It drops to last in line. Before it will pay for therapy, you have to go to your health plan first, be refused, and bring back the refusal in writing.
The catch
Nothing announces this change. It happens on your child’s third birthday, in the same month the eligibility rules get stricter.

Your health plan

Whoever covers your family — through work, through Covered California, or Medi-Cal.

What it owes
California law requires health plans to cover behavioural treatment for autism, and it becomes the main payer once your child turns three.
The catch
If your employer is self-funded — the company pays the claims itself and the insurance company only administers them — California’s law does not reach it. That is roughly four in ten Californians with job-based coverage, and closer to six in ten at large employers. Your HR department can tell you which you have, and it is worth asking before you need to know.

Your school district

From age three, once your child is found eligible for special education.

What it owes
Whatever your child needs in order to access their education — speech therapy, occupational therapy, aides, and so on, written into an education plan.
The catch
Only the school part. After school, at weekends, at home, and life skills that aren’t about learning — none of that is the district’s job, and it is allowed to say so.

The county

County social services, through a programme called IHSS.

What it owes
If your child needs watching around the clock to stay safe, the county can pay you — the parent — to do it, up to 283 hours a month. It is the largest paid help a California family can get.
The catch
The county is required to tell you this exists when it assesses your child. Most families are never told. You have to ask for it by name: Protective Supervision.

The single most important thing on this page

After your child turns three, three different organisations could be the one that pays, and each of them can point at the other two — correctly, under its own law. The health plan says it is educational. The district says it is medical. The regional centre says go and be refused by the other two first.

There is no referee. No organisation in California has authority over all three. The only person who can make them line up is you.

But one of these fights is winnable, and free

If the party saying no is your health plan, California will send the decision to outside doctors for you, at no cost. In 2024, of the roughly 5,200 families and patients who asked, about three in four ended up getting the treatment — and in one case out of five the plan simply reversed itself as soon as an outside reviewer was assigned. Ask for an Independent Medical Review. It is the one part of this system with a good record.

The one that does the work

Your provider

The therapist, agency or clinic your child actually sees.

What they do
The therapy. That is the whole of it.
The catch
They deliver only what somebody has agreed to pay for. They do not decide who pays, and it is not their job to go and get the funding for you. A provider telling you “we can’t do that” is usually telling you about the funding, not about your child.

The ones you go to when you are told no

Your service coordinator

The specific person at the regional centre assigned to your family. Also called a case manager.

On paper
California law makes this person responsible for getting the services in your child’s plan to actually happen, and for checking that they did.
In practice
They carry 62 children on average, and they work for the regional centre. When the reason a service didn’t happen is the regional centre’s own decision, this asks them to push against their own employer.

The state department

The Department of Developmental Services, which oversees all 21 regional centres.

What it does
Sets the rules, funds the centres, and hears appeals when you disagree with one.
The catch
It never serves a child directly. Nobody there knows your child’s name.

Free legal help

Disability Rights California, and the clients’ rights advocate attached to every regional centre.

What they do
Free advice about your rights, and sometimes they will represent you in an appeal.
The catch
They say so themselves: they can only take a limited number of cases. Most families get advice rather than representation. Call anyway — the advice is often the thing that was missing.

Worth knowing before you need it

Appeals work more often than families expect — when the argument is about whether your child is eligible, about half end with the family getting what was refused.

But there are two deadlines and they are not the same one. You have 60 days to appeal at all. You have 30 days — and before the change takes effect — to keep the services running while you appeal. A family that files on day 35 keeps its appeal and loses its services in the meantime.

And the honest limit: winning gets you a decision. That decision goes back to the same organisation that didn’t deliver in the first place. Nobody checks afterwards whether it happened. Put a date in your calendar and check yourself.

The one that just helps you

Your Family Resource Center

A small organisation staffed by parents of children with disabilities. There is one for your area.

What it does
Explains all of the above, in your language, from people who have been through it. Free. No diagnosis needed. They will talk to you even if your child turns out not to qualify for anything. It is the most useful and least known thing in this list.
The catch
It serves families of children under three. On your child’s third birthday the one organisation that has been helping you ages out — the same month everything else gets harder. Ask them, before then, who they would send you to next.

All of them, drawn against your child’s age

The whole thing in two sentences

BEFORE THREE

One organisation pays, one does the work, and one free parent-run group helps you understand it. It is confusing but it is not contested.

FROM THE THIRD BIRTHDAY

Three organisations might pay and each can point at the others; the rules for qualifying get stricter; the group that was helping you ages out; and the one person legally responsible for checking your child’s services actually happened is carrying sixty-two children.

You can see the result in California’s own figures. Among regional centre clients aged 3 to 21, about a quarter received no purchased services at all last year, and barely more than half of the money that was approved was actually spent. A plan that lists a service your child never got is not unusual. It is normal. That is worth knowing, because it means when it happens to you, it is not because you did something wrong.

The Handoffs Nobody Owns

The same map with the statutes and the state’s own numbers attached: six seams where one body’s duty stops before the next one’s starts, who stands in each now, and the five things California does not measure about itself. About 15 minutes.

Roles and duties here follow California’s published rules: the Lanterman Act and Welfare & Institutions Code sections 4647, 4659 and 4640.6; Education Code section 56043 for school timelines; the Department of Developmental Services Early Start FAQ of December 2025 for referral, cost and the 45-day timeline, and its Lanterman Act Appeals Information Packet for appeal deadlines; the Department of Managed Health Care 2024 Annual Report for the review figures; the Department of Social Services manual for Protective Supervision. Information, not legal advice — your Family Resource Center and Disability Rights California can tell you how any of it applies to your child.
