California · autism services

Three Pockets

Who handles what, and who pays — explained from zero, by following one child through the system. Each organisation is introduced the moment it appears, and every step says three things: who just showed up, who is paying, and who is responsible.

It has one goal: that when you finish, you can explain this system to someone else in your own words. Every date and number here was checked against California’s own statutes and reports; where something is the law and where it is merely what happens are kept apart.

Part one

First, why the system is shaped like this

If you remember one thing, remember this. Everything confusing that follows comes from it.

The services an autistic child needs are paid for out of three completely separate pockets, each with its own law, its own government department, and its own rules. None of the three answers to the others. No organisation in California has authority over all three.

So the most familiar scene a parent will meet is three organisations each saying “that is not ours” — and all three being legally correct.

Pocket 1 · the state

The regional centre

Under California’s Lanterman Act. Overseen by the Department of Developmental Services, DDS. Twenty-one regional centres statewide, all non-profits, spending state money to buy services.

Money: state budget → DDS → regional centre → provider

Pocket 2 · insurance

Your health plan

Under California’s SB 946: plans must cover behavioural treatment for autism. Regulated by DMHC. For lower-income families the plan is Medi-Cal, California’s Medicaid.

Money: your or your employer’s premiums → plan → provider

Pocket 3 · education

Your school district

Under the federal special-education law, IDEA. Overseen by the state education department, CDE, but districts are run by locally elected boards and are very independent.

Money: district budget → the school’s own therapists

The handover between pockets happens at a specific age. Before three there is essentially only Pocket 1. On the third birthday Pockets 2 and 3 arrive, and Pocket 1 drops to the back of the line.

That is why everyone tells you three is the hardest year. Your child did not change. The money changed pockets.

18 months

Something feels off

19 months · you make one call

The regional centre appears

Regional Center

You look up which regional centre covers your address and phone it: “I’d like an Early Start evaluation for my child.”

Early Start is California’s early-intervention programme for children under three, run by the regional centres. The law says: anyone may refer, including the parent; no doctor’s referral and no diagnosis are needed; from the day they receive the referral they have 45 calendar days to complete the evaluation and hold the first planning meeting; and the evaluation, the plan and the coordination are free at every income.

The bar is low: a 25% delay in any one area of development (language, for instance), or a known high-risk condition.

How, exactly
  1. Say this sentence: “I’d like to request an Early Start evaluation for my child.” They will ask the birthday, your address, and what worries you. Be specific: “doesn’t respond to her name, doesn’t point, doesn’t look at people.”
  2. Before you hang up, ask three things: “Does today count as day one of the referral?” “Is there a case or confirmation number?” “Roughly when will the evaluation be?” Put the date and the number in the folder. The 45 days run from the day they receive the referral, so that date matters.
  3. The evaluation is usually at your home or the centre’s office, one to two hours: a developmental specialist plays with your child and observes, and asks you questions. Bring: anything a doctor wrote, and videos you took of the behaviours — few things are more persuasive than a video.
  4. If the result is “not eligible,” you get it in writing. You have the right to dispute it. Call the Family Resource Center first (next stop) — they will walk you through it.
Money: none yet. This step is free.

21 months · within the 45 days

An evaluation, a plan, and “your person”

Service coordinator Family Resource Center

The regional centre evaluates Sam, then holds a meeting and writes a plan called an IFSP (Individualized Family Service Plan). It says which services Sam gets, how often, from whom, and to what goal. You are a legal member of that meeting and you sign the plan.

The regional centre also assigns you a service coordinator — from now on, your person there. In law (W&I §4647) they are responsible for “securing” the services in the plan and for “monitoring implementation … to ascertain that objectives have been fulfilled.”

You are also pointed to a Family Resource Center: run by parents of children with disabilities, free, no eligibility check and no diagnosis needed, and they will explain all of this in your language. Forty-seven of them statewide, contracted directly with DDS.

How, exactly
  1. Before the IFSP meeting: write down the three things that worry you most and bring the list. You may bring anyone — a friend, a relative, a parent from the Family Resource Center. You may ask for the meeting, and the plan, in your language.
  2. Get these written into the plan: for every service, the start date, who provides it (the agency’s name), and how many sessions a week for how long. A service with no start date has no start date. The law requires the meeting within 45 days; it sets no deadline for the first session — so you ask for a date and get it on the page.
  3. You do not have to sign what you disagree with. You can sign for the parts you agree with; those start regardless.
  4. After the meeting: get the coordinator’s direct line and email. The IFSP is reviewed every six months and rewritten yearly; in between, the coordinator does not call you. You call them.
  5. The Family Resource Center: if nobody mentions it, ask the coordinator: “Which is my Family Resource Center?” Get the number, call that week, and say “we just started Early Start and I’d like to introduce myself.”
Trap: the law sets a coordinator’s caseload at an average of 62 children (1:40 for under-fives), with up to 79 allowed for stretches of 60 days. “Securing the services” — one person, sixty-two children. You can picture it.

22 months · therapy starts

The provider appears

Provider / therapist

The plan says “ten hours a week of behavioural intervention.” The regional centre assigns an agency from its list of contracted providers (the vendor list), or you pick one from that list. That agency sends someone to work with Sam.

This is the only party in the whole system that actually does the work. Everyone else pays, plans, or adjudicates.

Money: regional centre → paid directly to the provider. You are not billed and you are not reimbursed. It does not pass through you.
How, exactly
  1. The coordinator gives you names, or you ask: “Can I have the vendor list for this service?” Then call each one — the four questions are below.
  2. Who shows up: usually a behaviour technician (RBT) for the daily sessions and a behaviour analyst (BCBA) who writes the programme and supervises. You may ask: “How often does the supervisor come? Will I meet the BCBA?”
  3. You can switch. Tell the coordinator “I’d like a different agency” — the plan gets a new name on it, and there is no penalty.
Trap: whether an agency can see Sam depends on three things that have nothing to do with Sam: whether it is contracted with your payer (on the regional centre’s vendor list, or in your plan’s network); which services, and how many hours, that payer approved; and whether it has staff right now. “We can’t do that” is, nine times out of ten, one of those three. Parents often hear it as “my child isn’t a fit.” It isn’t that.

2 years 6 months · preparing for three

The transition meeting: the school district appears

School district

California requires (17 CCR §52112) that before Sam is two years and nine months, the regional centre notify the school district and hold a transition meeting with a district representative present, and send the district a formal referral.

This is the only place in the entire system where the law requires two organisations to talk to each other directly — regional centre to district, one notice. Between every other pair of organisations, there is no line at all.

How, exactly
  1. At two and a half, ask the coordinator: “When is the transition meeting, and is the district coming?” If they say “it’s early,” say: “The rule is before two years nine months; I’d like the date set.” Ask for it in writing.
  2. What happens at the meeting: the district sends someone to explain its assessment process. Two things you can do on the spot: sign consent for the centre to send Sam’s records to the district; and hand the district representative a written request for assessment — that starts the district’s 15-day clock that same day (see the district stop below).
  3. Note: the district’s assessment and the regional centre’s age-three re-evaluation are two different things done by two organisations, and they can run at the same time.
Trap: as of February 2025 the state counted 6,929 children past their third birthday still on the Early Start books, up 22.5% in a year. The US Department of Education rated California’s under-three programme “Needs Intervention”, scoring zero on both transition measures because California’s own data were unreliable. So: whether this meeting actually happens, you watch. At two and a half, ask the coordinator one thing: “When is the transition meeting scheduled?”

The third birthday · three things happen the same day

The age-three handoff

First: the money changes pockets. The regional centre drops from “the payer” to “payer of last resort” (W&I §4659). The statute’s own words: for a child three or older, a regional centre “shall not purchase medical or dental services … unless the regional center is provided with documentation of a Medi-Cal, private insurance, or a health care service plan denial.” In other words, you go to your insurance first.

Second: the eligibility test changes. Before three it was a 25% delay in one area. At three Sam is re-evaluated: provisional eligibility for ages 3–5 needs significant limitations in two major life areas; full Lanterman eligibility needs substantial disability in three or more. Some children fall out here.

Third: the people who have been helping you age out. The Family Resource Center serves families of children under 36 months. On Sam’s third birthday, it graduates too.

How, exactly
  1. The regional centre’s re-evaluation: after three, eligibility is under Lanterman rules and usually needs a formal autism diagnosis from a psychologist or developmental paediatrician. If Sam does not have one, ask the coordinator at two and a half: “What does the age-three eligibility evaluation need? Do you arrange the diagnosis or do I?” The centre can arrange it and pay for it.
  2. Do not let the birthday open a gap: Early Start ends on the third birthday. The goal is that before that day the district’s IEP is in place and the centre’s re-evaluation is done. Both clocks are yours to push.
  3. On the birthday, three things: keep a copy of the centre’s new plan (the IPP) and the district’s IEP; ask HR whether the plan is self-funded; put the Family Resource Center’s “who comes next” answer in the folder.
Trap: nobody tells you any of these three in advance. The single most useful thing you can do at two and a half is ask the Family Resource Center: “After three you can’t help me — who should I go to next?”

3 years 1 month · going to the insurer

The health plan appears, and a letter

Health plan

The provider asks your plan to authorise treatment. California’s SB 946 requires plans to cover behavioural treatment for autism (ABA and similar). The plan may approve. It may refuse. If it refuses, you receive a refusal in writing.

Then — you take that letter back to the regional centre. Only when the regional centre sees the written denial, and judges that an appeal would not succeed, can it start paying again.

Why are you carrying the letter? Because the plan and the regional centre have no channel between them. They do not report to each other and they do not communicate. You are the courier.

How, exactly
  1. Find out what your plan is: the card. The company on the card is the plan; for Medi-Cal, the card names your managed-care plan (L.A. Care, Health Net, Kaiser, and so on). Employer coverage: ask HR, “fully insured or self-funded?”
  2. The agency requests the authorisation for you, but the plan will almost certainly want the formal diagnosis report and a doctor’s referral or prescription. Have both in hand and give them to the agency. Without a diagnosis the authorisation goes nowhere — which is why the diagnosis should be sorted before three.
  3. The refusal letter: the plan must state its reason and your appeal rights in writing. Photograph it, file it, keep the original. It is the key to every later step.
  4. The plan’s internal appeal: call the number on the card or write, and say “I am filing a grievance/appeal of this decision.” The plan must answer within 30 days (3 days if urgent). Only after this step — or after 30 days with no answer — can you go to DMHC for outside review.
Money (if approved): plan → paid directly to the provider. Still not through you. If there are copays or a deductible, a family under 400% of the poverty line may have the regional centre cover them (under three it must).
Big trap: if your employer is self-funded — the company pays claims itself and the insurance company only administers them — California’s law does not reach it; federal law takes precedence. About 41.7% of California private-sector employees with job-based coverage are in self-funded plans; at companies of a thousand or more it is 58.2%. You can ask HR one question today: “Is our plan fully insured or self-funded?”

3 years · in parallel

The district evaluation, and an IEP

You write the district a letter (in writing, dated): “I am requesting a special education assessment for my child.” The clock starts here:

Within 15 calendar days the district must give you an assessment plan to sign. You have 15 days to decide. From the day you sign, the district has 60 calendar days to finish the assessment and hold the IEP meeting. (School breaks longer than five days do not count.)

IEP = Individualized Education Program. The district’s written document setting out where Sam is now, this year’s goals, which services the school provides and how many minutes a week, what setting, and what classroom accommodations. It is legally binding. You are a legal member of the IEP team. It is reviewed at least yearly and fully re-evaluated every three years.

Note that Sam now has two plans at once — the regional centre’s (home and community) and the district’s IEP (school only). Two documents, two organisations, neither aware of the other. That is normal.

How, exactly
  1. Who to send it to: the district’s special education office (search “[district name] special education”). Send it by email so the date is on record, or hand it in and have them date-stamp your copy. Include: child’s name and birthday, your contact details, “I am requesting a special education assessment for my child in writing,” and the areas that worry you (language, social, behaviour).
  2. The assessment plan is a form listing the tests they intend to do. You may ask for areas to be added (“I am also concerned about sensory / motor”). Sign and return it promptly — the 60 days run from your signature.
  3. Who is at the IEP meeting: you, a district representative, a general-education teacher, a special-education teacher, the people who did the assessments. You may bring anyone. Ask for the meeting in your language and for an interpreter.
  4. If you disagree at the meeting: you can sign only the “attended” line and not “consent to all.” You can consent to part — the parts you consent to start regardless. The rest can go to mediation or a hearing.
  5. Get written in: minutes per week for each service, who provides it, where (in the classroom or pulled out), and when it starts.
Money: the district pays its own speech therapists, occupational therapists and aides. Not through you; not through the regional centre either.
Trap one: the 60 days run from the day you sign, not from the day you asked. If you sit on the form, the clock sits too. Trap two: if it is not written in the IEP, the district does not owe it. A teacher saying “we’ll keep an extra eye on him” at the meeting is worth nothing unless it is on the page. Trap three: the district owes only what school requires. After school, weekends, life skills — it may lawfully say those are not its job.

3 years 4 months · somebody said no

Appeals: three routes, three deadlines

DDS OCRA Disability Rights California DMHC

Who said no decides which route you take:

The regional centre said no (denied eligibility, cut a service) → you receive a Notice of Action. You have 60 days to appeal, and the appeal goes to DDS. But if you want your current services to continue while you appeal, you must file within 30 days, and before the change takes effect. File on day 35: the appeal survives, the services stop first. The numbers say appealing is worth it — in eligibility disputes, roughly 53% end with the family getting what was refused.

The health plan said no → first the plan’s internal appeal; then ask DMHC for an Independent Medical Review — free, decided by outside doctors, binding on the plan. Around 5,200 in 2024; 51% overturned, and in another 22% the plan reversed itself before the review even started. About three families in four got the treatment.

The district said no → the IDEA process: mediation, a complaint to the state education department, or a formal hearing (“due process”).

On all three routes there is free help: OCRA (a clients’ rights advocate attached to every regional centre — free, legally independent of it) and Disability Rights California (the state’s disability-rights legal organisation, free).

How, exactly
  1. Appealing the regional centre: the Notice of Action comes with DDS’s appeal form and address. Fill it in, send it (mail or email), keep proof of the date you sent it. The sequence: an informal meeting with the centre → optional mediation → a hearing. You may bring anyone.   3. Disputing the district: ask for mediation first (free, run by a neutral third party); if that fails, file for a due process hearing. Disability Rights California has free guides and a hotline.

3 years 6 months · you cannot keep this up

The county appears: the only money paid to you

County / IHSS

Sam needs someone watching around the clock to stay safe — runs out the door, has no sense of danger. California has a county-run programme called IHSS (In-Home Supportive Services) with a component called Protective Supervision: the county pays you, hourly, for the supervision you are already doing — up to 283 hours a month for the most severely affected. The parent can be the paid provider.

You apply through county social services, and you must ask for “Protective Supervision” by name.

How, exactly
  1. Where to apply: search “[your county] IHSS apply”; call the county IHSS line or apply online. Say: “I want to apply for IHSS for my child, including Protective Supervision.” The child needs Medi-Cal — most children with disabilities can get it through the “institutional deeming” route regardless of the parents’ income; ask the coordinator or the FRC about that.
  2. There will be a home visit. A social worker comes and asks questions. Describe the worst day, not the average day. Be specific about danger: “opens the door and runs toward the road,” “puts things that aren’t food in his mouth,” “gets up at night and wanders.” Say the words “Protective Supervision” and ask the worker to note them.
  3. A doctor has to fill in a form (SOC 821, the Protective Supervision needs assessment) confirming the child cannot judge danger because of a cognitive or mental condition. Take the form to the paediatrician or the diagnosing doctor.
  4. Once approved: you enrol as the “provider,” submit monthly timesheets, and the county pays you hourly into your account.
  5. If refused, or the hours are too few: appeal in writing within 90 days. Existing hours continue during the appeal. If you lose, you owe nothing back.
Money: county → paid directly to you. The only arrow in this whole picture that ends at the parent.
Trap: the county is required to tell you this exists at assessment. Almost no family is told. If refused, appeal: 90 days, benefits continue during the appeal, and if you lose you do not have to pay anything back — that last sentence is the one nobody knows.

Age 4 · it is in the plan, and it didn’t happen

Nobody owns this square

Sam’s regional centre plan says “ten hours a week,” but the agency keeps failing to staff it — six sessions in three months. You call the coordinator. They say they are following up.

In law this is exactly the coordinator’s job (§4647: “monitoring implementation … to ascertain that objectives have been fulfilled”). In practice they have sixty-two children, and their employer is the organisation that decides whether to pay.

California’s own figures: among regional centre clients aged 3 to 21, 24.7% received no purchased services at all last year — 66,618 people; and for that age group only 56.6% of the money that was approved was actually spent. “In the plan, not in real life” is the normal condition for one family in four, not your bad luck.

And the deeper point: of everything a regional centre is measured on, not one measure is “the service reached the child.” They count children identified, plans written, bilingual staff hired, staff trained.

How, exactly
  1. How to keep the record: each week, the hours the plan says versus the hours delivered. Ask the agency for its attendance or session notes — you are entitled to them.
  2. Escalating when the coordinator does nothing: first, email the coordinator and copy their supervisor (ask: “who is your supervisor?”). Second, file a §4731 complaint with the regional centre’s director, in writing, stating the service is not being delivered as planned; the director must answer in writing within 20 working days. Third, if the answer is unsatisfactory, take it to DDS within 15 working days — miss that window and the centre’s answer stands by default.
  3. Every step goes in the folder. That record is the whole of your evidence next time you appeal.
Trap: win your appeal and you get a decision on paper — which goes back to the same organisation that didn’t deliver the first time. Whether it then happens, nobody looks back to check. Only you, with a date in your own calendar.

Age 22

The cliff

The district’s obligation ends. Adult services are discretionary and rationed. National data: 26% of autistic young adults received no services at all in their early twenties; 58% had ever worked for pay after high school (92% for learning disability); 37% were neither working nor in education. About 80% of California’s autism caseload is under 22 — the cliff is not the edge of the system; it is the system’s shape.

DWhat we would build · nothingNot us, not now. The reader on this side of twenty-two is the autistic adult, not the parent — and someone needs to ask them what they want first. It is written here so that its absence is a decision rather than an oversight.

A card for each party

You have met all of them now. Here is one card each, in the same format, for comparing.

The regional centre

Regional Center · 21 statewide

A non-profit that buys services with state money. Where you live decides which one is yours.

Money from
The state budget, via DDS.
How to find it
The DDS website has a lookup by zip code; or search “regional center” plus your county. Write down its Early Start intake number.
You give it
A phone call. After three: the plan’s written denial.
It gives you
An evaluation; a plan (IFSP under three, IPP after); a service coordinator; under three, direct payment to the provider.
Law requires
Evaluate and meet within 45 days; notify the district before 2y9m; secure and monitor the plan (§4647); publish spending by ethnicity and language yearly and hold a public meeting on it (§4519.5).
It does not
Pay first after three; provide school-based services; talk to your insurer for you.
Trap
The role flips on the third birthday and nobody says so. Not one performance measure is “the service arrived.” One coordinator, 62 children.

The service coordinator

Service Coordinator / Case Manager

The specific person the regional centre assigns to you.

Money from
Employed by the regional centre.
How to find it
Assigned to you by the centre after the evaluation. Get their direct line and email; you are notified if they change.
You give it
Everything about your child; your phone calls.
It gives you
Writes the plan, arranges the agency, answers questions. In theory: tracks whether services happened.
Law requires
“Implementing, overseeing, and monitoring” every plan (§4647(b)).
Trap
62 children on average, up to 79. What they are asked to chase is often their own employer’s decision. No public data on this job’s turnover exists — the State Auditor told DDS to fix the staffing formula and four years on it is still “pending”.

The state department

DDS · Department of Developmental Services

The state agency that oversees all 21 regional centres.

Money from
The state budget.
How to find it
You do not normally deal with it. The appeal form and address are on the Notice of Action; website dds.ca.gov.
You give it
An appeal, when you disagree with a regional centre.
It gives you
A ruling. And statewide data reports.
Law requires
Fund, set rules for, audit the centres; hear appeals.
It does not
Ever serve a child directly. Nobody there knows Sam’s name.
Trap
The State Auditor told it to “establish standards for measuring consumers’ access to services by January 2023.” As of June 2026 that is still pending. It has admitted, in effect, that it cannot measure whether people are being served.

Your health plan

Health Plan · employer coverage / Covered California / Medi-Cal

The main payer after three.

Money from
Your or your employer’s premiums. Medi-Cal is state and federal money.
How to find it
The card: the company name and the member-services number are on it. For Medi-Cal, the card names your managed-care plan.
You give it
(Through the provider) an authorisation request.
It gives you
An approval; or a refusal letter — which you carry to the regional centre.

Responsible for the “needed for school” part.

The district’s own budget (state education funding plus local).

Part five

So what we intend to build: five things, each one questioned

The fix boxes above keep saying “the calendar,” “that page,” “the lookup.” They are only five things. Each is questioned below the way you would question it: what is it, is it an app, who fills it in, who sends it, what does the parent see, does it cost anything, does it exist, how would it fail.

One · A card

Used at: the “something feels off” stop

For the parent who is not in any system yet.

What is it?

A business-card-sized piece of paper. One sentence and one phone number on the front; a QR code on the back that opens The First Ninety Days. English, Spanish and Chinese versions. Also an image version, for forwarding in group chats.

Is it an app?

No. It is paper.

What does it say?

“You don’t have to wait for the doctor. You can request a free developmental evaluation for your child yourself, and they must do it within 45 days. Call: ___ (your regional centre’s Early Start intake line).” That is all.

What does the parent do?

Makes one call. The number on the card.

Where does it sit? Who hands it over?

On the table in a paediatric waiting room; at the Family Resource Center; forwarded as an image in a parents’ chat. Whoever hands it over says nothing; it just has to be there.

What do clinics and Family Resource Centers have to do?

Agree to leave the card on the table. One thing. It is what we go and ask for, one by one.

Does it cost anything? Who pays?

Free to the parent. We pay printing, a few hundred dollars.

What will we know about the parent?

Nothing. The card is one-way. At most we can see how many times the page behind the QR code was opened.

Does it exist?

No. A day to design, a week to print. The hard part is not the card; it is getting clinics to put it out.

How would it fail?

Clinics decline (not wanting to annoy their doctors); parents see it and don’t call; each regional centre has a different number, so cards are printed by area, and a wrong number is a wrong card.

Two · The calendar

Used at: coordinator, transition, third birthday, district, appeals, plan-versus-reality — seven of the eight A-type traps

The centre of the plan. A web page, plus a series of messages that arrive on the parent’s phone on the right days.

What is it?

A web page plus a series of messages. The parent fills in the page once; after that, messages arrive on her phone on specific dates. Each message says one thing: what should have happened by today; if it hasn’t, who to call and what to say.

Is it an app? Do I download something?

Not an app. Nothing to download. Open a web page, fill in four fields, close it. From then on the messages come to her; she never has to open anything again.

Who fills in the information?

The parent herself. Four fields: child’s birthday, county, insurance type (regional centre / employer plan / Medi-Cal / don’t know yet), and how she wants messages (text / email / WeChat). Sixty seconds.

Why not have the coordinator fill it in? Then every family would get it automatically.

Three reasons. One: if the coordinator enters it, the regional centre has handed a child’s details to a third party, which is governed by confidentiality law and needs a data agreement signed by the centre — months, and the negotiating partner is the very organisation we are routing around. Two: some messages take the parent’s side (“file within 30 days or your services stop”); if the centre entered the data, the parent would assume the centre sent the message, which makes that sentence strange — and a centre that dislikes the message can simply stop entering families, and the distribution is gone. Three: it gives the coordinator a task, and the point is that he has none. So: the parent fills it in, we send, under our name. The coordinator only hands over a card.

Who sends the messages? Under whose name?

We do, as Power Plus Partner. The parent knows who is talking to her and can reply “stop” at any time.

How do the messages reach the phone?

By whichever route she chose. Text needs a sending service (Twilio or similar, a few cents a message); email is cheapest; WeChat needs an official account. Text and email first.

Exactly which messages does she get, and how many?

About fifteen, spread over two years and more — not a daily nuisance. Two kinds.
Sent automatically, by age:
· On sign-up: confirmation, and when the first message will arrive.
· 2 years 6 months: the transition meeting must happen before 2y9m — ask the coordinator today when it is scheduled; three things happen at once in three months; ask the FRC who comes next; ask HR whether the plan is self-funded.
· 2 years 9 months: the district referral should have gone out — confirm; the age-three re-evaluation usually needs a formal diagnosis — is it arranged?
· Third birthday: Early Start ends today; check the IEP and the new plan; and if your child needs round-the-clock supervision, the county has a programme called Protective Supervision — keep the name.
Sent only when the parent presses a button: a few buttons on the page; she presses one on the day she did the thing —
· “I sent the district my assessment request today” → at once (three things to remember), day 15 (has the plan arrived?), day 60 after signing (has the IEP meeting happened?).
· “I received a Notice of Action today” → at once (thirty days, not sixty), day 20 (ten days left).
· “The plan said no” → at once (internal appeal first; check which regulator), day 30 (request the IMR).
· “I wrote to the plan today” → day 30 (any answer? none counts as a refusal).
· “I won my appeal” → day 30 (has the service started?).
· “I’ve called five and none can take us” → at once (your log is now evidence; send it to the coordinator; here is the sentence).

What if the child is already three when she signs up?

It counts from the birthday; dates already past are not sent; it starts from the current age. Sign up at three and a half and you get the button-triggered ones.

Does she have to reply? What if she doesn’t?

No reply needed. Messages are one-way. The buttons are there if she wants them; if she never presses one, she gets only the age-based messages.

What will we know about the child? Where is it stored?

We know: birthday, county, insurance type, a phone number or email, and which buttons she pressed. We do not know: the name, the diagnosis, the address, any medical information. It sits in our database. She can unsubscribe and ask for deletion at any time, and deletion means deletion.

Does it cost anything? Who pays?

Free to the parent. Our cost is message fees plus hosting — a few dollars per family per year.

What does the coordinator have to do?

Hand over a card. On it, a QR code and one sentence: “Scan this; it reminds you on every important date what to do.”

How does the parent find out it exists?

The biggest problem. Three routes: the coordinator hands over the card (he has a reason — her calls get shorter); the Family Resource Center hands it over (they are with the family the whole under-three period); our page.

Does it exist? How long?

No. Most of the message content already sits in the guide pages. To build: the sign-up page, the sending back end, the buttons, and final wording for the fifteen messages. Two to three weeks.

How would it fail?

Nobody knows it exists (distribution); a date or statute in one message is wrong — a day off is wrong, so every message needs a second pair of eyes; she changes her phone number; the messages get treated as spam.

Three · The “four questions” page

Used at: the “therapy starts” stop, and “everyone says no”

Already built. /autism/guides/four-questions

What is it?

A web page. Three things on it: the four questions to ask a provider before anything else (in knock-out order), what a good answer to each sounds like, and a call log.

How does the parent use it?

Opens it before dialling, asks in order, ticks the log and jots what they said. After ten calls the log shows at a glance who can take her, who has a waitlist, who cannot.

Where is the log stored? Can we see it?

In her own browser; switch devices and it is gone. We cannot see it. It is a record about her child — who refused, and why — and does not belong behind an address anyone could open.

Then how does the calendar know she has called five?

It doesn’t. She presses the “I’ve called five” button on the calendar page herself, and only then does the message go. Two separate things, joined by one press.

Does it cost anything?

No. It is on the site.

Does it exist?

Yes. Only distribution is missing — the calendar links to it at the “therapy starts” stop, and the FRC and the coordinator send it with the vendor list.

Four · The “which regulator is my plan under” lookup

Used at: the insurance and appeals stops

One search box. Nobody in California has built it.

What is it?

A web page with one search box. The parent types the company name from her insurance card and the page tells her which department regulates it, who to complain to if refused, and the number.

Why is it needed?

California splits insurance between two regulators: DMHC covers most health plans (mainly HMOs), CDI covers the rest (mainly PPOs). A complaint to the wrong one is wasted. There is no public place to check which is yours — today a parent learns she filed with the wrong one by filing with the wrong one.

What does the answer look like?

“Kaiser → regulated by DMHC. If refused: internal appeal first (30 days), then request an Independent Medical Review, 1-888-466-2219, free.” Or: “This one → regulated by CDI. Complaints: ___.”

Where does the data come from? Is it accurate?

DMHC’s and CDI’s own published lists of what they regulate; official. Two traps, though: one, the same company can have products on both sides (Blue Shield’s HMO under DMHC, its PPO under CDI), so the answer may be “depends whether your card says HMO or PPO.” Two, a self-funded employer plan is under neither — the card says Anthem but the employer pays the claims and Anthem only administers, so “regulated by DMHC” would be wrong. The search box therefore asks first: “Is your plan self-funded? If you don’t know, ask HR.”

How does the parent find it?

The “the plan said no” message carries the link; the four-questions page carries the search box.

Does it cost anything?

No.

Does it exist?

No. Pull the two lists into one searchable table: a week. Then refresh the lists quarterly.

How would it fail?

Lists go stale; the parent doesn’t recognise the name on her card (sometimes it is the administrator, not the insurer); the self-funded question isn’t asked first and the answer is wrong.

Five · The monthly question

Used at: the plan-versus-reality stop · type C · after the ten conversations

The only one of the five that asks the parent to tell us something. And the only one where the rules have to be written before the thing is built.

What is it?

Once a month the calendar sends: “Of the 40 hours in this month’s plan, how many actually happened?” The parent answers with a number.

How does she answer?

Reply to the text or email, or tap a link and type a number. Ten seconds.

What do we do with the number?

Two things. For her: three months on, when she escalates or appeals, that is her evidence, no remembering needed. In aggregate: if many families answer, we can see which agencies and which regional centres persistently fall short — something no public dataset in California holds. The State Auditor told DDS to build that measure; three and a half years later it has not.

Who can see her answer?

This is what has to be settled first. Options: only she can; anonymous totals are visible to others; whether agency names are shown. Each choice has different consequences, and whichever version the first parent sees is the only version there can be — changing it means going back to every one of them.

Could it be used against a particular agency?

To be settled. If yes, agencies will guard against parents; if no, the data is less useful. Not a technical question — a question of who we are.

Can she delete it?

She should be able to, and deletion should mean deletion. That one can be settled now.

Why does it come last?

Because the three questions above, settled wrong, cannot be unsettled. So: ten family conversations first, listening to how parents react to “if someone asked you this,” then the terms, then the question.

Does it exist?

No. And it shouldn’t yet.

Two things we will not do

Type D

The coordinator’s caseload?

Set by law and budget; not ours to change. The calendar entry above says what the parent does instead.

After twenty-two?

Not us, not now. The reader on that side is the autistic adult, and someone needs to ask them what they want first. Written here so that its absence is a decision, not an oversight.

Order: the card and the four-questions page can go out now; the calendar in two to three weeks; the lookup in a week; the monthly question after the ten conversations.

Part six

Five things to keep after you close this

One. The money comes from three pockets — the state, insurance, the school district — three laws, none answering to the others, and no referee.

Two. On the third birthday the money changes pockets: the regional centre drops to last, the plan and the district arrive, the people helping you age out. Nobody says so in advance.

Three. There are no lines between the organisations. The plan’s refusal letter reaches the regional centre because you carried it. You are the post office.

Four. The money does not pass through you — you neither pay nor receive — except the county’s one payment, which almost nobody is told about.

Five. Nobody’s job is to check afterwards that the therapy happened. One child in four has a plan that says one thing and a life that shows another. That is not something you did wrong.
